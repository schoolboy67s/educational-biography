Introduction

This is the educational biography of my mother, who grew up in India. Education and socialization in India differ greatly from America. Students in India face serious societal expectations tied with gender and belief systems, the reality of living on a low household income, strict rules in schools and harsh punishment, and systemic underfunding in her school.

Her first experiences of socialization were from her family, who were low-income. Although her parents always told her that she should find a good husband and dismiss her own dreams of becoming a nurse, they really did love her and wanted her to break the cycle of poverty and live an enjoyable life. Unfortunately, she had no siblings, so growing up having to always be there to help her parents with work was a struggle. They tried their best to make her childhood as an only child a good one. At school, her teachers said the same thing — even the female teachers.

The Cycle of Socialization

Harro's Cycle of Socialization describes how we are taught — through family, school, media, and community — to accept certain roles and beliefs without questioning them. My mother's story can be mapped onto each stage of this cycle. Below, I trace her journey and, where relevant, contrast it with my own.

Stage 1 — The Beginning

Born into a world already shaped

Before she could speak or understand, my mother was born into a society where girls were expected to become wives and mothers. Her family was low-income, and education for girls was seen as a path to a better marriage, not a career. She had no choice in these starting conditions.

Stage 2 — First Socialization

Family teaches the first lessons

Her parents told her that education mattered, but for a girl it came with conditions. Her father said education could never be taken away, but her mother warned her not to get "too educated" or no man would marry her. Her dream of becoming a nurse was dismissed. She learned that being a good girl meant being quiet, obedient, and focused on the home.

"Don't get too educated, or no one will want to marry you." — Her mother's warning

Stage 3 — Institutional & Cultural Socialization

School reinforces the message

In school, the same lessons were repeated. Boys were the future leaders; girls were the future wives. Teachers called on boys more often, praised them for solving problems, and gave them roles like class monitor. Girls were praised for being "quiet and working hard." Sports were for boys. The computer class in 9th grade was "not for girls." She was sent to home science instead, where she learned to cook and sew.

"This class is not for girls. You should take the home science class instead." — Her teacher, when she tried to sign up for computer class

Stage 4 — Enforcements

Rewards for conformity, punishment for resistance

Students who obeyed were rewarded with certificates, candy, and praise. Those who questioned were punished. My mother was once made to stand outside for talking to a friend during a lesson. Corporal punishment was normal — boys were hit with rulers or canes. Parents rarely intervened because complaining would bring shame to the family. The system enforced obedience through fear.

Stage 5 — Results

Internalized oppression

Over time, my mother became quieter. She stopped asking "why." She hid the parts of herself that didn't fit the idea of a "good girl" — her love of sports, her curiosity, her dream of being a nurse. She internalized the message that her value was measured by her marriageability, not her intelligence.

Stage 6 — Actions

The cycle continues — unless we interrupt it

Many people in my mother's life did nothing. They accepted the system because it was all they knew. Complaining was risky. Conformity was safer. The cycle perpetuated itself because no one challenged it. But there were exceptions — people who chose a different path.

Stage 7 — Change & Resistance

The Hindi teacher who saw her

Among all the strict and biased teachers, there was one exception. A Hindi teacher in 7th or 8th grade who never hit anyone and never made students stand outside. After my mother wrote a good paper, this teacher told her she had a gift with words. It was the first time a teacher had ever acknowledged her as someone with something valuable to offer. That moment stayed with her for decades.

Research Context

My mother's memories are not isolated. Government data and news reporting confirm that the conditions she describes — underfunded schools, corporal punishment, and gender discrimination — are part of larger systemic patterns in India.

Underfunded Schools: The Data

Source: ThePrint, "Gen Alpha is upset over poor school infra, shortage of teachers. Govt data shows what's wrong" (2026).

95,000

Schools without functional electricity.

5.42 lakh

Schools without functional computers.

1.39 lakh

Schools without a library.

The article explains that these gaps are not new. They are documented in the government's own UDISE+ report. My mother described sharing textbooks because the school couldn't afford enough copies. She described a cracked blackboard and no library. These numbers show that her experience was part of a national pattern, not an exception.

Corporal Punishment: Outlawed, Still Prevalent

Source: Al Jazeera, "A teacher's slap, student's death revive India's corporal punishment debate" (2026).

India banned corporal punishment in schools in 2009. But the practice continues. A 2018 study by the NGO Agrasar found that nearly 80 percent of low-income schoolchildren in one city reported being beaten several times a week. A majority of their parents approved of it.

My mother said that parents rarely intervened: "If the teacher hit you, you must have done something wrong." The article explains why — complaining could bring shame to the family, and school authorities often protected teachers. The law exists, but the culture has not caught up.

What This Means for Me as a Future Teacher

My mother's story is not just her story. It is a reminder of how powerful socialization is — how family, school, and community can teach a child what they are "supposed" to be before they have a chance to decide for themselves.

As a future teacher, I want to be the Hindi teacher in a student's life. The one who sees them. The one who says, "You have a gift." The one who does not hit, does not shame, does not treat one group of students as more worthy than another.

I also want to disrupt the biases I have absorbed. I grew up with more resources than my mother did. I had computers, libraries, and teachers who encouraged creativity. But I also absorbed messages — about who succeeds, who belongs, and what "normal" looks like. Those messages can be just as limiting as the ones my mother heard.

The universal message I hope to live by is this:

"Every student deserves to be seen, heard, and valued for who they are — not for who society tells them they should be."

This is what my mother's Hindi teacher did for her. It is what I hope to do for every student who walks into my classroom.
